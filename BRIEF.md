# Project Brief: The Affordability Gap

## Summary

The Affordability Gap is an independent educational, narrative-driven data story about why affordability pressures in Canada have changed over the past decade.

This is an interactive story, not a dashboard. It develops an argument through chapters, explanatory graphics, and carefully sourced evidence.

## Audience

Canadian citizens, journalists, policy analysts, researchers, and public-sector leaders.

## Core question

Why do affordability pressures feel different today than they did ten years ago?

## Core message

Housing, population growth, inflation, and wages are connected. Affordability pressures do not have a single cause, and their effects differ across the country.

## User need

Readers should be able to understand:

- How everyday prices and housing costs have changed
- How population growth and housing supply relate
- How inflation and wage growth affect purchasing power
- Why experiences differ between provinces
- Which indicators are useful to watch next
- Where the evidence comes from and what it can and cannot show

## Narrative structure

1. Canada feels more expensive
2. People and homebuilding move at different speeds
3. Inflation added pressure
4. Canada is not one story
5. What Canadians should watch next

Each chapter should advance the central argument. Avoid turning the story into a collection of unrelated indicators.

## Data approach

The official edition uses a locally cached source snapshot so the complete experience works without network access; the fictional prototype remains available for comparison. Clearly label illustrative values and show source metadata for official observations. Before using data, define each measure and validate its source, geography, unit, frequency, reference period, and transformation.

The story should explain association and context without claiming that one indicator alone proves causation.

## Design intent

Create a data-journalism experience with strong headlines, annotations, chapter transitions, and explanatory graphics. Keep the narrative in the foreground and use charts only when they make a relationship or change easier to understand.

Use accessible contrast, responsive layouts, clear source notes, and purposeful motion. Avoid dashboard conventions, including KPI grids, alert panels, and controls that interrupt reading.

## Success criteria

After reading, a first-time visitor can explain why affordability is challenging, identify the major contributing forces, describe why provinces differ, and name useful indicators to watch next.

## Disclaimer

This is an independent educational prototype. It is not an official Government of Canada product and does not provide policy advice.
